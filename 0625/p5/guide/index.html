About this tool

Every mark Cambridge IGCSE Physics Paper 5 examiners penalise — compiled from every examiner report since 2021

Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 Paper 5 (Practical Test) is a 40-mark paper sat in 1 hour 15 minutes across four questions: three short experiments and a planning question. Marks are awarded for taking and recording measurements with appropriate precision, drawing and interpreting graphs, applying physics reasoning to experimental data, identifying and explaining sources of error, and completing a planning exercise. Unlike written papers, the practical test has strict technique requirements: measurements must be recorded to the correct number of decimal places, best-fit lines must balance the points, and sources of error must be specific to the experiment. Examiner reports note that generic answers — “parallax error”, “human error”, “systematic error” without further explanation — rarely score.

This guide is built from CIE Examiner Reports, March 2021 to June 2026, across Paper 5 (variants 51, 52, 53) and Paper 6 (variants 61, 62, 63), which test the same skills in the same contexts. Frequently asked questions

IGCSE Physics Paper 5, quickly answered

What is Cambridge IGCSE Physics Paper 5?

Paper 5 is the Practical Test — a hands-on laboratory exam worth 40 marks (1 hour 15 minutes) where you use apparatus to take readings, record and process results in tables, plot graphs, draw conclusions and evaluate the experiment.

How long is IGCSE Physics Paper 5 and how many marks is it worth?

Paper 5 is 1 hour 15 minutes long and worth 40 marks. Candidates sit either Paper 5 (practical test) or Paper 6 (alternative to practical); both are worth 40 marks, and Paper 6 is 1 hour.

What is the difference between Paper 5 and Paper 6?

Paper 5 (Practical Test) is done in a lab with real apparatus; Paper 6 (Alternative to Practical) tests the same skills on paper, from described experiments. Schools choose one route for each candidate.

How do you revise for IGCSE Physics Paper 5?

Learn the mark-scheme habits — accurate readings, correctly headed tables, best-fit lines, valid conclusions and named sources of error — rather than only grinding past papers. This guide ranks the mistakes examiners flag most across the 2021–2026 reports.

Every rule, every common error, every examiner quote.

  • Precision — always record to the nearest millimetre

    Linear measurements must be recorded to the nearest millimetre — one decimal place in centimetres. Criticised in 9 of the 17 Paper 5 reports, March 2021 to June 2026. Recording to the nearest centimetre shows the ruler was not read properly.

    Examiner report: The instruction to measure h in centimetres to the nearest millimetre was sometimes not followed.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2025

    The rule: Record all lengths to the nearest mm. In cm: always one decimal place (e.g. 15.0 cm, not 15 cm).

    • Scores marks: 15.0 cm — to the nearest mm

      Does NOT score: 15 cm — only to the nearest cm

    • Scores marks: 2.0 cm — shows measurement was 20 mm

      Does NOT score: 2 cm — implies only measured to nearest cm

    • Scores marks: Record h as e.g. 4.7 cm

      Does NOT score: Round to 5 cm

  • Significant figures in calculated values

    Calculated answers should be given to 2 or 3 significant figures. Quoting a derived result to 5 or 6 sig figs (e.g. a density as 0.934721 g/cm³) is wrong — raw measurements from a ruler or balance cannot justify that precision.

    Examiner report: Numerical answers should be expressed clearly, to the appropriate number of significant figures and with a correct unit, where applicable.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2021 — all variants

    The rule: Aim for 2–3 significant figures in calculated answers unless the question specifies otherwise.

  • Units in table headings

    Every column heading in a results table must include the quantity name and its unit. Common failures: weight in g or kg (must be in N); density units wrong (correct: g/cm³ or kg/m³); T² written as s instead of s²; ratios (e.g. b/a) given a unit (ratios are dimensionless); heading missing the unit entirely.

    Examiner report: Common errors were not including units in the headings, incorrect units, e.g. weight in g and not N.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2021

    The rule: Every table column: quantity name / unit. No unit = mark lost. Ratios have no unit.

  • Never overwrite measurements

    Cross out the wrong measurement clearly with a single line and rewrite it next to the original. Overwriting creates ambiguity and the examiner cannot award a mark for an unreadable answer.

    Examiner report: Candidates should be encouraged to cross out completely and to re-write their answers so that there is no ambiguity.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2021

    The rule: Never overwrite a measurement. Cross it out clearly with a single line and rewrite next to it.

  • Cooling rate = temperature change, not final temperature

    If two containers start at different temperatures, comparing final temperatures is not valid. The fair comparison is the temperature drop (ΔT) over the same fixed time period. The March 2021 report credited final temperatures only where the initial temperatures were equal.

    Examiner report: Stronger candidates were aware that it is the temperature difference rather than the final temperature of the water which is the indicator of cooling rate.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, March 2021

    The rule: Cooling rate = temperature drop over a fixed time, not the final temperature reached.

  • Scale selection — fill the grid

    Awkward or too-small scales are criticised in 13 of the 17 Paper 5 reports. Use sensible scale increments: 1, 2, 4, 5, or 10 per division. Avoid 3 or 7 per division — these make accurate reading impossible. Start the axis at a value close to the lowest data point, not necessarily at the origin (unless instructed).

    Examiner report: A choice of scales which meant that the plotted points did not occupy at least half of the grid.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2021

    The rule: Plotted points must fill at least half the grid in both the x and y directions.

  • Plotting points — small and neat

    A plot too large makes the centre impossible to judge. Use a small neat cross, or a dot inside a small circle — no larger than half a small grid square.

    Examiner report: However, some candidates’ plots were too large and they produced blobs. They should be no larger than half of a small square on the graph.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2021

    • Scores marks: Small, neat cross or circled dot — no larger than half a small grid square

      Does NOT score: Large blobs or filled circles — the centre cannot be judged

    • Scores marks: Mark the centre of each point with a small clear symbol

      Does NOT score: Smudged or very thick pencil marks

  • Best-fit line — balance the points, never join them

    A best-fit line must have roughly equal numbers of points on either side along its full length. The line passes through the origin only if the physics guarantees it AND the data support it. A straight line not through the origin shows a linear relationship — not proportionality. The Paper 5 reports criticise dot-to-dot lines in 11 of the 17 series and lines forced through the origin in 8.

    Examiner report: There were many graphs where the attempt at a best-fit line was forced through the origin.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2021

    The rule: Do NOT draw the best-fit line as a dot-to-dot. Do NOT force it through the origin unless the physics demands it.

  • Axis labels — quantity / unit, both axes

    Both axes must show the quantity label and the unit: quantity / unit — for example, length / cm or resistance / Ω. Missing labels or units cost one mark per axis.

    The rule: Every axis: quantity name / unit. Missing either = mark lost.

  • Gradient calculation — large triangle, axis values

    Use a large triangle spanning at least half the line. Choose two coordinates that lie on the line (not plotted data points) and read precisely from the scale. gradient = Δy ÷ Δx — not Δx ÷ Δy. Always draw the construction lines so the examiner can see your method. Do NOT count grid squares — use the axis scale.

    Examiner report: A small but significant number of candidates tried to calculate a gradient by referring to the number of 2 mm squares on the graph paper rather than using their axis scale.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, November 2025

    The rule: Large triangle, coordinates from the line, Δy ÷ Δx using the axis scale.

  • Proportionality — the origin test

    A straight line on its own does not prove proportionality. To claim direct proportion, the line must pass through the origin AND you should confirm the ratio y/x is constant, or doubling x doubles y.

    Examiner report: Very few candidates realised that the line must pass through the origin to show a proportional relationship.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2021

    The rule: Proportional = straight line through the origin. Linear = straight line. These are not the same.

    • Scores marks: Straight line through origin → y is proportional to x

      Does NOT score: Straight line NOT through origin → linear, not proportional

    • Scores marks: Confirm: ratio y/x constant, or doubling x doubles y

      Does NOT score: "There is a straight line" without checking the origin

  • The experimental tolerance rule

    Whenever a question asks whether two experimental values can be considered equal within the limits of experimental accuracy, decide with a calculation, not by eye. Older reports (June 2021 to June 2022) described a grey zone: 5% or less equal, more than 10% different, and either answer accepted in between if you qualified it. Mark schemes from 2022 onwards use 10%, and recent questions state it.

    Examiner report: The idea of experimental tolerances, and whether two measured quantities are close enough to be considered equal was not well understood by the majority of candidates.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2021

    The rule: Use the limit the question gives (recent papers say "within 10 per cent of each other"; mark schemes since 2022 use 10% even when it is not stated). Quickest check, from the June 2025 report: smaller ÷ larger. 0.90 or more means equal; below 0.90 means not equal. Show the numbers, then write a yes or no that matches them, not the theory.

    • Scores marks: Ratio 0.94 → "Yes, they are equal within the limits of experimental accuracy", with the calculation shown

      Does NOT score: Just stating "they look close"

    • Scores marks: Ratio 0.86 → "No, they are too far apart to be considered equal"

      Does NOT score: Writing what the theory predicts when your numbers say otherwise

  • Conclusion methods are not predictions

    A conclusion method states HOW you will use your data to decide. A prediction just states what you expect. Only the method scores. Writing a prediction instead of a method is named in 15 of the 17 series.

    Examiner report: The question did not ask for a prediction. Some candidates wrote a prediction, but no explanation of how to reach a conclusion.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2021

    The rule: A conclusion method states how you will USE your data. A prediction states what you expect. Only the method scores.

    • Scores marks: "Plot initial temperature vs rate of cooling. If a straight line through the origin: cooling rate ∝ initial temperature."

      Does NOT score: "The temperature would decrease more slowly." — prediction, not method

    • Scores marks: "Compare the time for each heater to reach boiling. Shortest time = most efficient."

      Does NOT score: "I think the copper heater will be fastest."

  • Direct proportion — ratio or doubling, not just "increases with"

    "As θ increases, r increases" only proves positive correlation. To claim direct proportion you must show either: r/θ is constant for all values, or doubling θ doubles r.

    Examiner report: The most common incorrect answer was that r is directly proportional to θ because as θ increases r increases.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, November 2025

    The rule: Constant ratio OR doubling argument. "Increases with" alone is not enough.

  • Generic answers never score

    A source of error must name a specific feature of THIS experiment. If your answer could apply to any experiment, it won't score. "Parallax error", "human error", "experimental error", "the ruler wasn't accurate" — all rejected.

    Examiner report: Most candidates gave generic examples of poor experimental technique, such as parallax error and not reading the ruler correctly, despite the wording of the question.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2021

    The rule: Sources of error must name a specific feature of this experiment. If it could apply to any experiment, it will not score.

    • Scores marks: Pins have width — exact position of the ray is uncertain (ray-tracing)

      Does NOT score: Parallax error — too vague: parallax in what?

    • Scores marks: The mirror has thickness — reflecting surface is behind the glass

      Does NOT score: Human error — never credited

    • Scores marks: The clay block has irregular faces — dimensions vary along the block

      Does NOT score: The ruler was not accurate

    • Scores marks: Fixed resistor prevents the test wire heating up and changing R

      Does NOT score: Experimental error

  • "Carefully carried out" → equipment limitations only

    If the question states the experiment was carefully carried out, you cannot cite technique errors. Only equipment limitations score: inaccurate test load, non-uniform metre rule, non-uniform spring, and so on.

    Examiner report: It was expected that these would refer to sources which the experimenter would have no control over, such as an inaccurate value of the test load or a non-uniform metre rule or spring.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, March 2022

    The rule: If the question says "experiment was carefully carried out": only cite equipment limitations, not technique errors.

  • Ray tracing — view the bases of the pins

    View the BASES of the pins when aligning them, not the tops. For the pin experiment, "carry out in a darkened room" earns nothing (June 2021, November 2021, November 2024). It is credited only when the question asks how to see an image clearly, such as finding the sharpest image from a lens (June 2024, November 2023). It was not credited when the question asked how to set up a lens experiment (March 2024). Place the pins well over 5 cm apart: pins drawn exactly 5.0 cm apart risked being just too short (November 2021). Angles are measured from the normal, not the surface. n = sin i / sin r is dimensionless — no unit.

    The rule: Ray tracing with pins: "view the bases of the pins" (also thin pins, thin lines, pins well over 5 cm apart). Only write "darkened room" when the question is about seeing an image clearly.

  • Percentage uncertainty grows for small measurements

    A reaction-time error of 0.2 s is 20% of a 1 s period — but only 2% of a 10 s total. This is why we time 10 oscillations, not 1. The same principle applies to length: measuring a 5 cm object has higher % uncertainty than a 50 cm object with the same ruler.

    Examiner report: The correct answer focused on the fact that when taking smaller measurements, the effect of the uncertainty in the measurement (due to the resolution of the measuring device) is greater. There is a larger percentage uncertainty in smaller measurements.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, November 2025

    The rule: Smaller measurement = larger % uncertainty. Measure more, not less.

  • Timing oscillations — 10 in one go, divide by 10

    Time 10 complete oscillations as ONE continuous measurement, then divide by 10. Do NOT time 10 individual oscillations and average them — that compounds the reaction-time error.

    Examiner report: Some candidates appeared to refer to 10 separate timings of one oscillation giving an average result, not realising that a single time for 10 oscillations is the technique involved.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2021

    The rule: Time 10 complete oscillations as ONE continuous measurement. Divide by 10. Do NOT time 10 individual oscillations and average them.

    • Scores marks: Start at equilibrium. Count 10 complete oscillations. Stop. T = total ÷ 10.

      Does NOT score: Time one oscillation, reset, repeat 10 times, average. — WRONG.

    • Scores marks: Use 5 or 10 oscillations to reduce the proportional reaction-time error

      Does NOT score: Use 1 oscillation and claim it is accurate

  • Do not copy apparatus from the question

    List only ADDITIONAL apparatus that you are bringing in. Copying out the list the question already provided wastes time and scores nothing.

    Examiner report: A significant number of candidates copied the list of apparatus and other information given in the question. This was unnecessary and often introduced a vague explanation of the investigation.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, June 2021

    The rule: Only list ADDITIONAL apparatus not already named in the question. Copying given information scores nothing.

  • Variables — the exact language required

    State the IV explicitly in words. State the DV explicitly with the instrument used to measure it. Name at least 2 control variables with the method of control. Implying through method or table does not score.

    Examiner report: The variable needed to be clearly stated to obtain credit, and not just implied in the method, table or analysis.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, November 2025

    The rule: State the IV, DV and control variables EXPLICITLY in text — naming, units, and how each is varied / measured / controlled.

    • Scores marks: IV: wire diameter d / mm, varied by using wires of different diameters.

      Does NOT score: "Change the wire" — too vague: what property?

    • Scores marks: DV: resistance R / Ω, measured using a voltmeter and ammeter.

      Does NOT score: "Measure the resistance" — no instrument named

    • Scores marks: Controls: temperature T (room temperature); wire material (same type).

      Does NOT score: "Keep everything else the same"

  • Minimum 5 values, repeated and averaged

    The method must include measurements for at least five values of the IV. For each value, repeat the measurement and take the mean. "Repeat the experiment" is weaker than stating you will repeat each measurement at each IV value.

    The rule: At least 5 values of the IV. Repeat each measurement. Calculate the mean. All three must be stated.

  • Table of results — quantity / unit, never instrument names

    Column headings: quantity name / unit — e.g. current / A, not "ammeter reading" or "I" alone. Include a column for the IV. Derived quantities (e.g. rate of cooling) need their own column and unit. Weight in N, not g or kg.

    Examiner report: Most candidates completed the table successfully, but a significant number did not fill in the column headings.

    Source: CIE Examiner Report, November 2025

    The rule: Table headings: quantity / unit, never instrument names.

  • Graph — confirming the relationship

    State explicitly which graph confirms the relationship. Proportional (y ∝ x): plot y vs x, straight line through origin. Linear (y = mx + c): straight line, any intercept. Where the relationship involves a square, plot that quantity directly (for example T² against d).

    The rule: Always state: which graph, what shape confirms which relationship.

  • Conclusion method — not a prediction

    State how you will USE your data to reach a conclusion. Do not state what you expect to find. Writing a prediction instead of a method is named in 15 of the 17 series.

    The rule: Conclusion method = how you use the data. Prediction = what you expect. Only the method scores.

    • Scores marks: If the graph of cooling rate vs initial temperature gives a straight line through origin, cooling rate ∝ initial temperature.

      Does NOT score: I predict the cooling rate will increase with temperature.

    • Scores marks: Compare the time for each heater to reach boiling. Shortest = most efficient.

      Does NOT score: The electric heater will be more efficient.

  • Circuit diagrams — voltmeter parallel, ammeter series

    Voltmeter: always in parallel with the component being measured. Ammeter: always in series. Variable resistor: rectangle with arrow — not the thermistor symbol. Voltmeter must span the full series combination, not just one component within it. Include a switch.

    The rule: V in parallel. A in series. Include the switch. Variable resistor ≠ thermistor symbol.

  • Routinely-omitted practical details

    Spring: record initial unloaded length so extensions can be calculated. Cooling: state a fixed cooling time (e.g. 5 min), not "until room temperature". Refraction: list BOTH protractor AND ruler. Lens: move the screen, not the lens or object. Diagrams: springs must be shown hanging from a clamp stand, not in mid-air.

Test yourself on the things examiners actually mark.

  • You measure a length with a ruler. Which is correctly recorded?

    • 12 cm
    • 12.0 cm
    • 12.04 cm
    • 0.12 m

    Linear measurements must be recorded to the nearest mm — one decimal place in cm. "12 cm" is only to the nearest cm.

  • A scale of "3 units per division" is:

    • Fine — as long as the plots fill the grid
    • Wrong — should never be used
    • Risky — examiners flag 3 or 7 per division as awkward
    • Acceptable only on the x-axis

    Use 1, 2, 4, 5 or 10 per division. 3 and 7 make accurate reading hard and were explicitly flagged in multiple series.

  • A best-fit line should be:

    • Drawn dot-to-dot through every point
    • Forced through the origin
    • A single straight line with roughly equal points on each side
    • The line connecting the first and last point

    Roughly equal points on either side, along the full length. Dot-to-dot lines and lines forced through the origin are the two best-fit faults the reports name again and again.

  • Two values differ by 3%. The correct conclusion is:

    • "They are too different to be equal"
    • "They agree within experimental accuracy"
    • "More data is needed"
    • "The experiment failed"

    A 3% difference is well within 10% (ratio about 0.97), so the values agree within experimental accuracy. Show the calculation.

  • Two values differ by 14%. The correct conclusion is:

    • "They agree within experimental accuracy"
    • "They are too far apart to be considered equal"
    • "Close enough to be equal"
    • Either — it depends

    A 14% difference is more than 10% (ratio about 0.86), so the values are too far apart to be considered equal.

  • In ray-tracing, the BEST precaution is:

    • Carry out in a darkened room
    • View the bases of the pins, not the tops
    • Use thicker pins
    • Use a thicker pencil for the ray

    For the pin experiment, "darkened room" earns nothing (June 2021, November 2021). The credited precaution is viewing the BASES of the pins. A darkened room only earns credit when the question is about seeing an image clearly, as in the lens experiment.

  • For a pendulum, you should time:

    • One oscillation, repeat 10 times, average
    • Half an oscillation and double it
    • 10 complete oscillations as one continuous measurement, then divide by 10
    • As many as you can in 60 seconds

    10 oscillations timed in one continuous go reduces the proportional reaction-time error. Timing individually and averaging compounds the error.

  • A weak source of error is:

    • "Pins have width — the ray's position is uncertain"
    • "The mirror has thickness — reflecting surface is behind glass"
    • "Parallax error"
    • "The clay block has irregular faces"

    "Parallax error" is generic. Examiners want a feature specific to THIS experiment. Any answer that could apply to any experiment fails.

  • Which is the strongest statement of an independent variable?

    • "Change the wire"
    • "Use different wires"
    • "The independent variable is the wire diameter d / mm, varied by using wires of different diameters"
    • "Measure the wire"

    State the IV explicitly: named, with unit, with how it is varied. Implying it through the method does not score.

  • How many values of the IV should be measured?

    • At least 3
    • At least 5, each repeated and averaged
    • Exactly 10
    • As many as the apparatus allows

    At least 5 values, each measurement repeated, and the mean calculated. State all three.

  • "I predict the spring will stretch more with heavier loads" — this is:

    • A valid conclusion method
    • A prediction — does not score for the conclusion mark
    • Acceptable if backed by data
    • Required to introduce the method

    A prediction states what you expect. A conclusion method states how you will USE the data. Only the method scores — the reports name it in 15 of the 17 series.

  • A calculated density of "0.934721 g/cm³" should be:

    • Left as is — more digits = more accurate
    • Rounded to 2 or 3 significant figures
    • Quoted to 4 decimal places
    • Converted to kg/m³

    Raw measurements from a ruler/balance cannot justify 6 sig figs. Aim for 2–3 sig figs in calculated answers.

  • To claim direct proportionality from a graph, you need:

    • Any straight line
    • A straight line passing through the origin
    • A curve through the origin
    • A straight line on log axes

    Straight line alone = linear. Straight line through origin (with ratio y/x constant) = proportional. The two are not the same.

  • The voltmeter in a circuit measuring a resistor should be:

    • In series with the resistor
    • In parallel with the resistor
    • In parallel with the ammeter
    • Either works

    Voltmeter ALWAYS in parallel with the component. Ammeter ALWAYS in series. Common circuit error to lose easy marks on.

  • A column heading of "Mass / g" is used for weight readings. This:

    • Scores — mass and weight are interchangeable in everyday use
    • Loses a mark — weight must be in N
    • Is fine if the experiment is on Earth
    • Only matters in Q4

    Weight is a force — must be in newtons (N). Recording it in g or kg loses the unit mark. Flagged in multiple series.

After every practice paper, tick off what you got right.

  • Measurement and Recording

    • Every length is recorded to the nearest mm (one decimal place in cm)
    • All table column headings have a quantity name and a unit
    • Weight is in N. Density is in g/cm³ or kg/m³
    • T² is labelled in s² (not s). Ratios have no unit
    • Calculated values are given to 2–3 significant figures
    • No overwriting — any changes are crossed out and rewritten clearly
    • Cooling rate = temperature drop over a fixed time, not the final temperature

  • Graph Work

    • Plotted points occupy more than half the grid in both directions
    • Scale increments are 1, 2, 4, 5, or 10 per division (not 3 or 7)
    • Both axes have a label and a unit
    • Plotted points are small, neat crosses or circled dots — no blobs
    • Best-fit line is a single straight line, not dot-to-dot
    • Best-fit line is NOT forced through the origin unless physics requires it
    • Gradient triangle is large; construction lines drawn; coordinates read from the line
    • If proportionality is claimed: line passes through origin AND ratio y/x is constant
    • Gradient calculated as Δy/Δx using axis scale values, not grid square counts

  • Conclusions

    • When comparing two values: percentage difference is calculated and stated
    • Equal-or-not judged with a calculation: smaller ÷ larger, 0.90 or more → "equal within the limits of experimental accuracy"
    • The yes or no matches your numbers, not what the theory predicts
    • Conclusion method states how to USE the data (not a prediction)
    • Direct proportion justified using ratio or doubling argument — not just "increases with"

  • Sources of Error

    • Sources of error are specific to this experiment (not "parallax" or "human error" alone)
    • If question says "experiment was carefully done": only equipment limitations cited
    • Ray tracing with pins: "view the bases of the pins"; "darkened room" only when the question is about seeing an image clearly
    • Ray tracing: pins well over 5 cm apart (exactly 5.0 cm risks being just too short)
    • Timing: 10 oscillations timed as one continuous measurement, then divided by 10

  • Planning (Q4)

    • No apparatus copied from the question — only additional items listed
    • IV stated explicitly: named, with unit, with how it is varied
    • DV stated explicitly: named, with unit, with how it is measured
    • At least 2 control variables named with method of control
    • At least 5 values of the IV specified in the method
    • Repeat readings at each IV value stated; mean to be calculated
    • Table headings: quantity / unit (not instrument names)
    • Graph axes stated; conclusion method stated (not a prediction)
    • Circuit diagrams: voltmeter in parallel, ammeter in series, switch included
    • Essential setup detail included (initial spring length, fixed cooling time, etc.)
